Golf, nothing more

The cold is not what is costing you distance

November 2026. Every winter the same advice comes round: play a softer ball, keep one in your pocket, expect to lose yards. Most of it is aimed at the wrong problem.

What the cold actually does to the ball

Trackman put the figure at roughly one yard of carry for every ten degrees Fahrenheit of temperature change. Play at 40 degrees instead of 80 and the ball itself costs you about four yards.

Four yards. That is the whole effect everybody worries about, and it is less than the difference between the middle of the face and half an inch towards the toe.

The pocket trick does not rescue it either. A test by Golf Laboratories found a ball cannot be warmed enough to matter, because it takes on the outside temperature almost immediately. And warming a ball deliberately during a round is against the rules anyway, so the most you can do is start with a warm one and watch it cool on the first tee.

What is actually costing you

The jacket. Golf Digest's advice for cold rounds is to plan on at least half a club more, and a full club if your swing is restricted, and almost all of that is the clothing rather than the ball. An extra layer across the shoulders shortens your backswing, and a shorter backswing costs real yards, not four.

So if you want your winter distance back, the order is: thinner layers that still keep you warm, then a club more, then stop thinking about the ball.

The ball costs you about four yards. The coat costs you half a club. Everyone argues about the first one.

The real winter problem is grip

Cold grips go slick and hard, and wet cold grips are worse. That is where winter rounds are actually lost: not in carry, but in the two or three swings where the club moves in your hands and you do not commit.

Which brings up the most misunderstood piece of kit in the bag. Rain gloves are not there to keep your hands dry. They are made of an absorbent, non-woven material that uses the water: it bonds your hand to the glove and the glove to the grip. The wetter they get, the more they hold, which is why some players soak them on purpose before they start. They come as a pair because you want both hands locked on, and they work in sticky summer heat as well.

Clubs living in a cold car boot are worth a thought too. Steel shafts react to cold more than graphite does, and grips can harden and in extreme cold crack. Nobody has put a yardage number on that, so treat it as a reason to bring the clubs inside rather than a reason to buy anything.
