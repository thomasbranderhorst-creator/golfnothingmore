The 7 wood is taking over. Most of us have not noticed.

Golf, nothing more · October 2026

Somewhere between your 3 wood and your 5 iron there is a gap, and for most of us it is filled by whatever came in the set we bought. A 4 iron we cannot launch. A hybrid we never quite trust. For a long time that was simply the deal: long clubs are hard, get on with it.

That is changing, and it is changing from the top down.

What a 7 wood actually is

About 21 degrees of loft, which is the same as a lot of 4 irons. The difference is not the number on the sole, it is everything behind it. A bigger head lets the weight sit low and far back, so the ball leaves higher and the club is kinder when you miss the middle. The shaft is longer than a comparable hybrid, which buys back some speed.

Put those together and you get the thing tour players are actually after: a high flight with a steep landing angle. A ball that stops on a green from 200 yards instead of skidding through the back of it.

The numbers say the amateurs have not followed

MyGolfSpy pulled figures from Shot Scope, who track what golfers genuinely carry rather than what they say they carry. The split by handicap:

HandicapCarrying a 7 wood
1 to 105%
10 to 157%
16 and above10%

One in twenty single figure players. That is a long way behind the professional game, and a long way behind the manufacturers, who between them put out seven different 7 woods for 2026.

What our own group carries

So we asked. We put the question to our Facebook group on 22 October: 5 wood, 7 wood, 4 iron or a hybrid, what fills that gap? It drew 336 comments. We read 312 of them that named at least one club and counted what people said they carry.

The result does not look much like the tracked data above.

Club namedShare of 312 answers
7 wood60%
5 wood45%
Hybrid39%
9 wood or higher21%
4 iron18%

Most answers named more than one club, so the column adds up to more than 100. Nearly three in ten carry a 5 wood and a 7 wood together rather than choosing between them.

Two things stood out. The first is that 21% named a 9 wood, an 11 wood or in one case a 13 wood. If the 7 wood is the trend, a fifth of the people answering have already gone past it. The second is the 4 iron at 18%, which was the lowest of the four options we offered.

Now the honest part, because the number is too good to leave unqualified. This is not a survey of golfers. It is a count of the people who chose to answer a post about the 7 wood, and anyone who owns one has more reason to comment than someone who does not. Read it as a measure of who turns up to the conversation, not of what sits in the average bag. The gap between 1 in 20 in the tracked data and 6 in 10 here is the interesting part, and most of that gap is who is doing the talking.

What it costs to find out

This is the part that keeps people from trying one. A new 7 wood is not a small purchase, and you are buying it to answer a question you cannot answer in a shop.

Callaway list their Quantum Max fairway wood at $399.99 new. The same club, pre-owned through Callaway Golf Pre-Owned, starts at $249.59 at the time of writing. That is roughly a third off a club you are buying precisely because you are not sure about it yet.

If you are going to experiment, experiment second hand. A 7 wood either fits the gap in your bag or it does not, and you will know inside one round. There is no sense paying full price to find that out.

Should you put one in the bag?

Not automatically. If your 4 iron or your hybrid already gets the ball up and stops it where you aim, leave it alone. The 7 wood solves a specific problem: not enough height and not enough stopping power, from a distance you keep facing.

If that is you, the things worth watching on a launch monitor are carry, spread, spin between 4,000 and 5,000 rpm, and a landing angle steeper than 45 degrees. Distance on its own is not the point. A club that goes further and runs off the back of the green has not helped you.
